# I Winged It Into IT Risk

*Almost 3 Years in IT Risk: What I Learned, Earned, and Am Taking With Me*

---

I'll be upfront: when I stepped into the IT Risk Engineer role, I had little to no idea what real risk work looked like. I knew audits, I knew security operations, I knew IAM — but risk management as a discipline? I was mostly winging it at the start.

For most of my career, audit season meant one thing: brace yourself. Someone from a regulator or internal audit would come knocking, and I or my team would scramble to pull together evidence, explain our controls, and hope we had everything in order. I was on the receiving end. That was just how it worked.

Then I joined a global bank as an IT Risk Engineer, and everything shifted.

"The other side of the table" isn't exactly right though — because I wasn't the auditor. I wasn't the one coming in to test and challenge. But I was no longer the one scrambling to answer either. I became the person making sure others didn't have to scramble. I sat in the middle: translating risk frameworks for DevOps teams, coaching application owners on what good evidence actually looked like, and sometimes walking through controls myself when it came to that. Part guide, part coordinator, part internal safety net.

---

## A Different Kind of Pressure

Coming from a leading local money remittance company, I had spent years in IT operations, then system administration, then leading Identity and Access Management. By the time I transitioned, I had a solid foundation. But to be honest, it wasn't the typical background for an IT risk role at a global financial institution. A lot of my colleagues came in with more recognized backgrounds, bigger firms, more traditional risk pedigrees. I came from operations.

I remember wondering early on if I was out of my depth.

What I quickly realized: the operations background wasn't a liability. It was actually my edge.

---

## What the Role Actually Looked Like

IT Risk Engineer sounds like it could mean a lot of things. In practice, my team sat between the business and the auditors. We helped squads — DevOps teams, application owners, IT custodians — understand control requirements, gather proper evidence, and prepare for both internal and external scrutiny.

The work covered a wide range of areas:

**Risk Assessments** — full assessments for apps going live for the first time, periodic refreshes, and targeted assessments when there were major changes. Sometimes it was a deep dive across the whole risk profile; other times it was scoped to a specific control.

**Business Impact Analysis** — understanding what happens to the business if a system goes down, and making sure that reality was documented and accounted for in continuity planning.

**SOx IAM Controls** — rigorous testing cycles around identity and access management. Coordinating with key control testers and auditors, reviewing evidence, ensuring controls were not just compliant on paper but actually effective.

**Vulnerability Management** — I wasn't the one patching servers or fixing code, but I was heavily involved in the management side: overseeing scan results, supporting security hardening efforts, helping prioritize which vulnerabilities needed immediate attention versus which ones warranted an exception process. This also touched secure SDLC and coordination with DevOps teams.

Across all of this — Lifecycle Management, Change Management, Platform Security, IT Resilience and more. A big part of the job was helping squads improve their evidence quality. Not just checking a box, but making sure the evidence actually demonstrated that a control was working and that real risk was being managed.

---

## Certifications Along the Way

The role pushed me to formalize what I was learning, and each certification mapped directly to something I was experiencing on the ground.

**CISSP** came first as the role demanded broader security depth. It was challenging, but it validated that I belonged in the room — not just as an operations guy who crossed over, but as someone who could hold a security conversation at any layer.

**CRISC** (Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control) followed — it gave me the framework vocabulary I didn't come in with. Suddenly the structure behind what I was doing daily had a name and a discipline.

**Blue Security Champion** sharpened how I thought about embedding security into delivery pipelines, which directly fed into the secure SDLC work with DevOps teams.

And **AI-900** was pure curiosity — keeping an eye on where the technology was heading and what it might mean for the risk landscape down the line.

None of these felt like resume items. Each one felt like catching up to what I was already doing.

---

## The Mindset Shift I Had to Make

Early on, when a DevOps team struggled to provide proper evidence or missed a deadline, my instinct was to give them the benefit of the doubt. I understood where they were coming from. Their priority was delivering applications and managing incidents. Audit prep wasn't on their critical path.

That empathy was useful. It helped me communicate with technical teams in a way that didn't feel adversarial. But I had to grow past just sympathizing with delivery pressure. The risk controls exist for a reason. A bank's security posture depends on people actually taking them seriously, not just appearing to.

The balance I found: understand the reality of the teams you work with, advocate for practical controls, but don't let empathy become an excuse to let real gaps slide. That balance is harder than it sounds, and I'm still refining it.

---

## Exceeding Expectations Without the "Right" Background

In almost every performance evaluation over those almost three years, my rating came back as exceeding expectations. That still surprised me each time.

I think what helped was bringing genuine operational context to the risk conversations. When I talked to a DevOps engineer about a vulnerability or a control gap, I wasn't just citing a policy. I understood the environment they were working in. I could ask the right questions. I could recognize when a proposed control would be impractical versus when a team was just pushing back on inconvenience.

A more traditional risk pedigree gives you frameworks and structure. Operations gives you judgment. Ideally you'd have both, but you can build frameworks. Judgment takes time.

---

## The Work I Ended Up Loving

When I started, I thought I'd be most engaged by the IAM work — that was my background. But over time, I found myself most energized by vulnerability management, security hardening, and secure SDLC controls.

There's something satisfying about the tangible nature of that work. A vulnerability is a real, specific thing. Hardening a platform produces a measurable result. Working with developers to shift security earlier in the delivery process — that's where risk stops being theoretical and starts being engineering.

That pull was telling me something. Every time I sat with a DevOps team walking through a control or a hardening requirement, I wasn't just helping them comply — I was genuinely curious about what they were building and how it worked. The risk lens made me a better partner to them. And somewhere along the way, I realized I wanted to be on that side of the work too.

So when the opportunity came up within the same company to step into a DevOps Engineer role, it felt like the right next move. I'm not leaving risk behind — I'm bringing everything it taught me into how I build, deploy, and think about security from the inside. Not just building securely, but keeping it that way. The almost three years in IT Risk didn't just make me a better risk engineer. I think it's going to make me a better DevOps engineer.

---

## The People Made It

None of the technical growth would have meant as much without the people. I was placed in two squads over those almost three years, and in both, I found colleagues I genuinely connected with, spanning time zones and countries.

Our global lead set the tone early on, and it stuck with me: family and health come first. Not productivity. Not utilization. Not your ticket count. That kind of clarity from leadership changes how you show up, and it changed how I thought about what sustainable work actually looks like.

The goal wasn't to be the most prolific risk engineer in the squad. It was to take your work seriously, help the squads assigned to you become genuinely more secure, and be someone people trusted.

I think I did that. I'm grateful for everyone who helped me figure out how.
