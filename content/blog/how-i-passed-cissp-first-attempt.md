# I Passed the CISSP on My First Attempt. Here's What Actually Worked.

*How I approached one of cybersecurity's hardest exams — without burning out, without cramming, and without guessing my way through it.*

---

On June 17, 2024, my CISSP exam stopped at question 100.

I knew what that likely meant. I'd read enough about the adaptive format to understand that finishing early was usually a good sign. But knowing something intellectually and believing it emotionally are two different things. I sat there for a moment, breathed, and hit submit.

I passed.

What followed was a wave of exhaustion, disbelief, and then — slowly — genuine pride. Not just because I cleared one of cybersecurity's most respected certifications, but because I'd done it with a strategy I actually trusted.

This post is that strategy.

---

## Who I Was Before This Exam

Before moving into IT Risk and Cybersecurity, most of my career was built in IT operations and Identity and Access Management (IAM). I spent years doing the unglamorous but foundational work: managing access lifecycles, enforcing least-privilege controls, supporting infrastructure security, and navigating regulated environments where every change needed documentation and every exception needed justification.

When I started preparing for CISSP, that experience gave me a head start in certain domains — IAM, Security Operations, Software Development Security, and Communication and Network Security felt familiar. But other areas? Cryptography, Risk Management, Asset Security — those required real effort.

Here's the thing: the domains I struggled with most turned out to be the ones most relevant to my current work in IT Risk. Preparing for CISSP didn't just help me pass an exam. It filled in gaps I didn't fully know I had.

---

## How Long I Actually Prepared

Three to six months total, but the intensity varied enormously.

The early months were deliberate low-effort. I listened to review videos primarily to understand three things:
- What I already knew
- What was genuinely unfamiliar
- Where my professional instincts might mislead me on exam questions

The real preparation happened in the final one to two months. That's when I got structured.

**Typical schedule:**
- Weekdays: one to two hours
- Weekends: two to three hours

I also run regularly, and I used that time well — easier runs meant I could listen to YouTube or Udemy review content without losing the pace. This wasn't cutting corners. It was fitting preparation into a real life, which meant it stayed sustainable.

One thing I'd tell any CISSP candidate: tell the people around you what you're doing. I talked to my wife early about the time commitment. That single conversation made the whole preparation period smoother. This kind of exam doesn't just take your hours — it takes your headspace. The people who live with you will notice.

---

## The Study Method That Made the Difference

My approach was built around Pete Zerger's CISSP Exam Cram framework, and I'd recommend it to almost anyone:

1. Watch review videos to understand the domain
2. Take a practice assessment
3. Review every question you got wrong — or answered without confidence
4. Read the corresponding sections of the Official Study Guide
5. Retake practice questions and repeat until the domain feels solid

I worked domain by domain, not chapter by chapter in one long linear read. The breadth of CISSP material is one of the things that trips candidates up. Reading everything sequentially can create the illusion of progress without showing you where you're actually weak.

Practice questions were my diagnostic tool. They told me exactly where to spend time next.

---

## The Habit That Separated My Preparation: Tracking More Than Correct and Incorrect

Most people track which questions they get wrong. I tracked something broader.

I kept a spreadsheet that logged questions where:
- I answered correctly but felt like I guessed
- I was genuinely torn between two options
- I chose correctly but couldn't explain *why*
- I kept making the same mistake on a particular concept

This mattered because a correct answer isn't always evidence of understanding. CISSP questions are designed to test reasoning, not recognition. My goal wasn't to learn a question bank. It was to be able to think through an unfamiliar question under pressure and arrive at the right answer.

Alongside the tracker, I built notes on concepts I consistently missed, and I used existing mind maps to understand how topics connected across domains.

By the time I was within a few weeks of the exam, I was consistently scoring 85–90% across all domains. That became my confidence signal: not a gut feeling, but a measurable one.

## The Resources That Helped Me (And What Each One Did)

No single resource deserves full credit. Each served a specific purpose.

**For deep understanding:**
The *CISSP Official Study Guide, 9th Edition* by Chapple, Stewart, and Gibson was my reference anchor — but I never read it cover to cover. I used it for targeted reading after practice questions or videos flagged a weak area. That kept the reading purposeful.

**For domain review and structure:**
Pete Zerger's *CISSP Exam Cram* YouTube series was central. I watched the full series more than once. His framework became the spine of my preparation.

Destination Certification's MindMaps by Rob Witcher helped enormously when I needed to connect concepts or get above the detail level. When the forest was hard to see for all the trees, these maps helped.

The *Official Study Guide Audio* was useful for reinforcement without sitting at a desk — good for commutes or winding down after a long day.

**For the right exam mindset:**
This is where many candidates underinvest, and it costs them.

*Think Like a Manager* by Luke Ahmed is essential. CISSP doesn't reward the most technically precise answer. It rewards the most appropriate answer given business context, risk priorities, and governance realities. Understanding that distinction changed how I approached every question.

*Why You Will Pass the CISSP* by Kelly Handerhan reinforced the same idea. She has a way of making the mindset shift feel intuitive rather than forced.

One line from Luke Ahmed stayed with me during the harder preparation days:

> *"The harder you study, the more you lessen the fear of failing."*

Simple. But it's true.

**For the final stretch:**
- Andrew Ramdayal's *50 CISSP Practice Questions: Master the CISSP Mindset*
- Prabh Nair's Coffee Shots and exam prep content
- *The Memory Palace* by Prashant Mohan
- Digital flashcards from Pete Zerger and Destination Certification

## What the Exam Actually Felt Like

I won't discuss specific content — that's not the point, and it wouldn't help anyway.

What I can say: it felt nothing like practice tests.

The adaptive format created a particular kind of psychological pressure. You answer a question, you submit, and you move on. You don't know how you're performing. You can't go back. Every question demands a decision and then asks you to release it.

Many questions didn't feel clean. Multiple answers seemed plausible. That's not a flaw in the exam — it's the point. CISSP is testing whether you can make sound judgments under ambiguity, not whether you can recall the textbook definition of a term.

The mindset work I'd done made the difference here. When you've practiced choosing the most appropriate answer over the most technically direct one, that reasoning becomes more automatic.

When the exam stopped at 100 questions, I sat quietly for a moment before submitting. I'd done everything I could. The result was in.

The confirmation came. I'd passed.

That evening I celebrated with family, good food, and a few drinks. I'm not someone who often marks personal milestones, but this one earned it.

## Three Things I'd Tell Anyone Preparing Now

**1. Imperfect preparation still moves you forward.**
You will miss questions you thought you understood. You will have days where your confidence drops. You will also miss study days entirely — because life happens, because you get sick, because some weeks simply don't cooperate. None of that means you're failing. What matters is that you get back up. Show up the next day, pick up where you left off, and keep moving. Progress is not a clean streak. It's the pattern of returning.

**2. Trust the hours you've put in.**
At some point before the exam, you have to make a decision: trust your preparation or spend the remaining time feeding anxiety. The breadth of CISSP material makes it almost impossible to feel fully ready. If you've been consistent, doing the targeted review, tracking your weak areas — trust that. The alternative is a spiral that doesn't help anyone.

**3. Bring the people around you into it.**
Certifications extract real time and real energy from your life. The people you live with absorb that. Being honest with them about what you're doing, why it matters, and what you need — that's not a soft concern. It's practical. My wife's patience and support were part of why this worked.

## Where This Fits in a Bigger Picture

Passing CISSP wasn't the end of anything. It was a cleaner, more connected foundation to build from.

My background in operations and IAM gave me technical grounding. My work in IT Risk gave me a control and governance perspective. CISSP brought those threads together and gave me a shared language for the work.

As I continue building toward cloud security, DevSecOps, and security automation, the CISSP mindset stays relevant: understand the risk, think about the business context, and build security that is both sound and practical to operate.

That's the kind of professional I'm still working to become.
